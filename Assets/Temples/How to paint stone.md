# How to paint stone

Think of each limestone block as a simple readable shape first, then build the illusion of stone through a series of controlled passes:

>silhouette → base color → large lighting → edge planes → limestone variation → pits → cracks → chips → selective highlights → cleanup.

That order matters. It keeps the artwork readable even when you’re still learning.

### Start with a small limestone palette

Before drawing anything, make a little palette off to the side. Don’t constantly pick new colors from the color selector. For a warm Caribbean/Yucatán-inspired limestone, try something approximately like this:

| Purpose | Example |	Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deep outline | #403E39 | Exterior silhouette, deepest cracks
| Dark shadow | #625F56 | Bottom/side planes
| Warm shadow | #858074 | General shadow
| Base limestone | #B7AE9C | Main stone color
| Warm base | #C9BCA3 | Color variation
| Light limestone | #DDD2BA | Lit surfaces
| Bright edge | #EEE5D2 | Select highlights
| Mossy stain | #7E8460 | Optional environmental staining

Doesn’t have to be exact hex values, but the relationship:

>dark gray-brown → gray-beige → beige → cream → warm off-white.

Avoid pure black for most outlines and pure white for highlights. Both tend to look harsh.  
Also notice that my darkest colors aren’t simply “darker beige.” They’re cooler and grayer. That gives you much more attractive stone.

### PASS 1 — Draw the silhouette

Brush: hard-edged opaque brush with pressure controlling size, but little or no opacity variation. Think “digital ink brush” rather than pencil, airbrush, or watercolor.

For now, don’t texture anything. Draw one rectangular temple block. Don’t make it geometrically perfect:
- Make one corner slightly higher
- Bow one edge outward a little
- Flatten another corner
- Introduce a tiny irregularity

Ancient cut limestone should still clearly read as a rectangular architectural block, but not as a computer-generated rectangle.

Instead of black, use your deep gray-brown: #403E39. Make the silhouette fairly chunky. Lean toward a stronger outline than you initially think you need.

### PASS 2 — Fill the base limestone color

Create a layer underneath your outline. Fill the entire block with your base limestone: #B7AE9C

At this point your block should be incredibly boring. It should be: nice silhouette + flat beige fill.

### PASS 3 — Establish the major planes

This is probably the most important step. Before painting “stone,” paint the shape of the block. Imagine your light comes from the upper-left. The top plane gets lighter. The front gets the base color. The bottom/right-facing planes get darker. You don’t need literal 3D perspective. You’re simply communicating thickness.

Brush: large hard or semi-hard opaque brush. Not textured. You want large deliberate shapes.

Paint the top-facing areas with: #DDD2BA  
Paint the right/bottom-facing areas with something around: #858074

You now have only 3–4 colors on the entire rock. And it should already look somewhat dimensional. If it doesn’t, don’t proceed to texture yet. Fix the large forms first.

### PASS 4 — Add the cartoony edge treatment

This is where it starts looking like game art. Real limestone doesn’t conveniently highlight all of its edges. Cartoon limestone does.

Brush: smaller hard brush.

Take your lighter cream: #EEE5D2

Trace selected portions of the upper-left edges, not the entire block. So you might put:
* bright cream along 60–70% of the upper edge
* a little highlight on the upper-left corner
* short highlights along some chipped surfaces
* dark accents along the lower-right edge

This creates the chunky carved game-stone appearance you’ve been seeing in your generated temple assets.

Important: Don’t make the highlight a perfectly straight line. Break it.  
Instead of:  
────────────  
do:  
──── ──  ─────  
Stone edges are imperfect.  

### PASS 5 — Introduce limestone color variation

Now we’re finally going to make beige look like limestone. Create a clipped layer above the base.

Brush: large, low-opacity, irregular/cloudy brush. Something with uneven edges is useful here. Sponge-like, chalky, cloudy, granular—anything that produces broad irregular patches. Opacity around 10–25% is plenty.

Paint some broad areas slightly warmer: #C9BCA3  
And some slightly cooler/darker: #9D9A8E  

Think:

+----------------+
|     warm       |
|   .........    |
| ....    cool   |
|       .....    |
| warm           |
+----------------+

You’re not drawing identifiable shapes.

You’re breaking up the uniform color.

This is extremely important:

Your texture should usually be much lower contrast than your cracks, edges, and silhouette.

If your texture becomes the first thing you notice, it’s too strong.

PASS 6 — Create the characteristic limestone texture

Limestone usually benefits from three different scales of texture.

Don’t try to create all three with one brush.

A. Large mottling

You’ve already done this.

Large, subtle cream/beige/gray variations.

⸻

B. Medium irregular patches

Use a:

small-to-medium chalky or dry-brush texture.

Low opacity.

Add scattered areas of:

* warm beige
* pale gray
* slightly darker gray-beige

But don’t cover everything evenly.

Think clusters.

Something like:

 .  ..        .
     ...  .
 ..        ...
       .
   ...       .

Avoid:

. . . . . . .
. . . . . . .
. . . . . . .

Nature clusters.

Procedural-looking dots don’t.

⸻

C. Tiny pores

Now take a:

tiny hard round brush or sparse stipple brush.

Use your shadow color.

Add tiny pits.

But instead of random noise everywhere, make little groups:

 •
    • •
       •
 • •
  •

Some pits can simply be dots.

Some should be tiny crescents:

(
C
•

A crescent is especially useful because it implies a tiny cavity:

dark interior + tiny light edge.

For a larger pore:

1. Make a tiny dark oval.
2. Add a cream highlight along its upper-left rim.

Instant stone cavity.

This technique scales beautifully to game art.

⸻

PASS 7 — Add cracks

Now you get to draw cracks.

Create a separate layer.

Use a:

small hard brush with pressure-sensitive size.

Use your dark shadow color—not pure black.

Start cracks at places where stress makes sense:

* corners
* chips
* existing pits
* block seams
* impact areas

Don’t start every crack randomly in the middle.

A convincing crack might go:

       |
       |
      /
     /
    |\
    | \
       \

Notice that it branches.

But don’t make it a lightning bolt:

/\/\/\/\/\/

That immediately looks artificial.

Crack technique

Draw the main crack first.

Make it:

thick → thin → thinner

as it travels.

Then add one or two smaller branches.

Then stop.

Less is better.

⸻

PASS 8 — Give cracks depth

Here’s a really useful trick.

After drawing the dark crack, add a very thin light line along the lower or upper edge depending on your lighting.

For example:

dark crack
   ↓
  \
highlight
   \

The dark line represents the cavity.

The light line represents the tiny exposed stone edge catching light.

Suddenly:

line → actual crack in rock.

Don’t highlight every crack. Reserve it for larger ones.

⸻

PASS 9 — Chip a corner

This is easier than it looks.

Suppose you currently have this:

+-------------+
|             |
|             |
|             |
+-------------+

Erase part of the corner:

+-----------\
|            \
|             |
|             |
+-------------+

But don’t make one smooth diagonal cut.

Make it irregular:

+---------_
|          \_
|            |
|            |
+------------+

Now you’re exposing a new rock plane.

Fill that exposed plane with your lighter or darker limestone depending on its orientation.

For an upper-left chip, it might catch light:

#DDD2BA

Then add:

* dark line where the broken plane meets the original surface
* bright cream on one exposed edge
* 1–3 little pits inside the newly exposed area

Now the corner looks broken instead of erased.

⸻

PASS 10 — Make the block edges damaged

Don’t leave the outer silhouette perfectly smooth.

Use your eraser or transparent brush to make tiny bites:

_________  ___
         \/

or:

|
|
|_
  |
__|

Keep these relatively small.

A common mistake is making every edge incredibly jagged.

Ancient cut stone should still read as:

rectangular block → damaged over time

rather than:

random natural boulder.

That’s an important distinction for temple architecture.

⸻

PASS 11 — Add edge erosion

Limestone exposed to water can have beautifully rounded, softened edges.

Use a small textured brush with your lighter limestone color.

Break up the sharp edge:

original:
──────────────
weathered:
─── ~──  ─~───

Then underneath some of those highlights, add tiny darker indentations.

You get:

cream edge → tiny dark cavity → cream edge

which creates that porous, water-eroded limestone appearance.

⸻

PASS 12 — Add environmental staining

This will connect the limestone to your underwater temple.

Create another clipped layer.

Use a:

soft irregular brush at very low opacity.

Add muted green:

#7E8460

Primarily around:

* bottom edges
* cracks
* block seams
* pits
* places water would collect
* underneath overhangs

Don’t paint green uniformly over the rock.

Think:

algae lives in recesses.

So your flat surfaces remain cream/beige while cracks gradually become green-gray.

That contrast will look great.

⸻

PASS 13 — Put moss into selected cracks

Now use a:

small textured brush or clustered foliage brush.

Use a muted olive green.

Put tiny bits around perhaps 10–20% of the cracks.

Not every crack.

Something like:

stone stone
────┐
    │ 🌿
    │
────┘

For the cartoony style, you can exaggerate this slightly.

Use:

dark olive base → medium green → tiny yellow-green highlight.

Three values are enough.

⸻

PASS 14 — Final outline cleanup

Now return to your silhouette layer.

This is important.

Texture and painting will probably have weakened your original shape.

Use your dark gray-brown and reinforce:

* underside
* right side
* deep chips
* major cracks
* areas where one block overlaps another

But don’t outline every internal texture mark.

Think hierarchy:

thickest: exterior silhouette
medium: major block divisions/chips
thin: cracks
very subtle: texture

That hierarchy is one of the biggest differences between clean game art and an illustration that feels visually noisy.

⸻

PASS 15 — Final highlight pass

Create a layer at the very top.

Take a small hard brush and your warm off-white.

Add a handful of highlights.

Maybe:

* one strong upper-left corner
* two or three chipped edges
* a few pore rims
* part of the top edge
* one exposed crack

That’s it.

Don’t highlight everything.

Highlights are valuable because they’re scarce.

⸻

Brush types I’d keep available

You really only need about five brush behaviors for this entire workflow.

Brush type	Use
Hard opaque round/ink	silhouette, cracks, edge highlights
Large hard/semi-hard brush	base colors and lighting planes
Chalk/dry textured brush	limestone mottling
Sparse stipple/speckle brush	pores and tiny surface variation
Soft irregular/cloud brush	subtle staining and color variation

And an eraser with a hard or slightly textured edge for chips.

You absolutely do not need dozens of specialty brushes.

In fact, while learning, fewer brushes will probably improve your art because you’ll learn how the shapes work instead of hoping the brush creates the texture for you.

⸻

The most important mental model

When you’re drawing your block, think of the information in this order:

1. What shape is it?
Rectangle.

2. Which direction does it face?
Top/light, front/mid, side/dark.

3. What material is it?
Warm porous limestone.

4. How old is it?
Cracks, chips, softened edges.

5. Where has water affected it?
Pits, erosion, staining.

6. Where has life taken hold?
Moss/algae/coral.

Don’t reverse that order.

If you start with algae, cracks, dots and coral, you’ll spend an hour beautifully decorating a rock that doesn’t have convincing volume.

A good beginner rule

At normal game-view size, you should be able to turn all texture layers off and still say:

“Yep, that’s a chunky limestone temple block.”

Then turn the textures back on and it becomes:

“That’s an old underwater limestone temple block.”

That’s exactly what you want.

Correct: one completely solid, flat color.

If your base is #B7AE9C, fill the entire interior with exactly that color. No pressure variation, texture, airbrushing, blending, mottling, or subtle shading yet.

So at the end of Pass 2 you should literally have something resembling:

One beige shape surrounded by your dark outline.

I’d use either Krita’s fill tool or a fully opaque hard brush. There is no artistic painting happening in this pass.

That’s intentional because you’re separating two questions:

“What shape is this object?” comes first.

“What is this object made from?” comes later.

What is a clipped layer?

Your understanding is essentially correct.

Suppose your base layer contains this shape:

        XXXXXXX

      XXXXXXXXXXX

You create another layer above it and clip/inherit alpha from the stone.

Now you could wildly paint this:

///////////////////////////////
///////////////////////////////
///////////////////////////////
///////////////////////////////

but only the portion overlapping your stone becomes visible:

        ///////

So yes:

It prevents your painting from appearing outside the stone’s existing shape.

In Krita, you’ll encounter Alpha Inheritance for this sort of workflow. Krita’s implementation has some nuances compared with Photoshop-style clipping masks, but conceptually that’s what I’m describing.

Layer opacity or brush opacity?

For Pass 5, I’d start with brush opacity.

For example:

* Layer opacity: 100%
* Brush opacity: perhaps 10–20%

Then softly build the mottling.

Why?

Because you might make one stroke:

10%

then another:

10%

and another:

10%

and overlapping strokes gradually accumulate.

So yes, your understanding is exactly right:

You’re not really “blending” the paint. The semi-transparent paint allows the colors underneath to remain visible.

Imagine painting a translucent warm beige over gray-beige.

Instead of replacing it, you’re tinting it.

Once you’re finished, you can also reduce the whole layer’s opacity if you decide you overdid it.

That’s another advantage of using a separate layer.

About the hard brush creating dark circles

This is an excellent observation.

Yes.

If you use a large hard round brush and just click:

●

you’re going to get a polka dot, not a hole.

The trick isn’t really softness. It’s size and construction.

For tiny pores at game scale, literally a tiny dark dot can work because your eye interprets it as a pit.

But for anything larger, construct the hole.

Instead of:

●

think:

  light rim
     ↓
   ◜───
  ( DARK )
   ───

So you could:

1. Draw a tiny irregular dark oval.
2. Don’t make it perfectly circular.
3. Put a tiny cream highlight on its upper-left rim.
4. Potentially add a medium shadow around one portion.

Suddenly the “dot” reads as an indentation.

Should the brush be soft?

Generally no for the actual pore.

A soft airbrush produces:

fuzzy stain

A hard brush produces:

physical cavity

For your cartoony game style, the latter is much better.

You can use a textured/chalky edge if you want irregularity, but I’d avoid fuzzy airbrush pores.

And yes, keep many of them very small. At actual game scale, some might literally only occupy a handful of pixels.

Pass 7 — Thick → thin cracks

Pressure is one way, yes.

With a pressure-sensitive brush:

more pressure → thicker
less pressure → thinner

So one natural stroke can taper.

But don’t feel obligated to draw the whole crack in one elegant stroke. As a beginner, that can actually be harder.

You can draw:

━━━━━
     ━━━
       ━━
         ━

and manually reduce your brush size as you work.

Or draw the crack, then erase/taper its ending.

The important principle isn’t how you produce it. It’s the resulting hierarchy:

origin
████
  ███
    ██
      █
       ·

The crack generally loses visual weight as it travels away from its origin.

And the tiny branches should usually be thinner than the primary crack.

One more distinction that I think will help you enormously

There are four different things we’re doing that can easily feel like “texture” when you’re starting out:

Lighting tells me the shape:

This side points toward the light.

Color variation tells me the material:

This isn’t uniformly manufactured beige plastic.

Surface texture tells me the physical surface:

This is porous limestone.

Damage tells me the history:

This stone has been sitting here for centuries.

So when you’re looking at your drawing and wondering what to do next, ask which problem you’re solving.

If the block feels flat, don’t add texture. Fix lighting.

If it looks like beige plastic, add color variation and pores.

If it looks brand-new, add cracks, chips and erosion.

If it looks like it has never been underwater, add staining and biological growth.

That separation will make this process much easier to learn than trying to “paint rock” all at once.
